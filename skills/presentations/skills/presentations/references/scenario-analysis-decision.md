# Scenario: analysis and decision

## Audience task

Help an informed audience compare evidence, understand tradeoffs, and make a
specific decision. The deck should reduce uncertainty rather than manufacture
confidence.

Lock the decision, criteria, time horizon, baseline, and uncertainty before
drawing. Every recommendation must point to evidence or an explicit assumption.
Counter-evidence belongs near the claim it limits.

## Narrative

Frame the decision, establish criteria, show the evidence and alternatives,
surface uncertainty, recommend a path, then make the next decision explicit.
Put decisive evidence before the recommendation. Use an appendix for audit
detail, not for facts required to understand the choice.

Useful page jobs include decision frame, baseline, driver analysis, option
comparison, sensitivity or scenario range, recommendation, conditions, and
next action. Do not force all of them into one fixed sequence.

## Density and rhythm

Alternate compact decision frames with denser evidence pages. Preserve common
scales across comparable charts. Give exceptions and uncertainty enough space
to be read. A live deck may stage a complex driver chain; a reader deck must
remain self-contained without the animation.

## Visual carriers and archetypes

- annotated trend, variance, waterfall, or distribution chart;
- option comparison on shared criteria and a common visual scale;
- driver tree, causal chain, or sensitivity map;
- evidence table with one decision-relevant row or threshold highlighted;
- recommendation page with conditions, trigger, owner, and next action;
- scenario range rather than a single false-precision forecast.

Charts should encode a relationship, not decorate a number. Tables work when
exact values or criteria matter. Diagrams work when causal structure matters.
Use sourced imagery only when the physical context changes the decision; do not
use a generic market image as the evidence carrier.

## Visual grammar

Use restrained surfaces, straight geometry, explicit axes, thin dividers, and
one high-contrast decision role. Prefer direct labels, aligned baselines, and
consistent units. Use boxes only when they encode comparable alternatives or
bounded states. Connectors must carry direction, dependency, or causality.

Typography should separate claim, evidence annotation, and source. Spacing
should make the decision path scannable without turning every observation into
a card. Motifs may mark thresholds or selected options, but must not become a
second legend.

## Avoid

Dashboard walls, ornamental KPI cards, incomparable scales, decorative arrows,
equal emphasis on every metric, recommendations before evidence, hidden
uncertainty, and confidence language unsupported by the source.

## Review questions

- Can the audience state the decision, criteria, and uncertainty after a scan?
- Does each visual change the decision, or merely decorate a number?
- Are counter-evidence and assumptions visible before the conclusion?
- Is the requested action explicit, bounded, and supported?
- If the page feels generic, should the claim/evidence or carrier change before
  palette and styling are adjusted?

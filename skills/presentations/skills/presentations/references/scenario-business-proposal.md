# Scenario: business proposal

## Audience task

Help a sponsor understand a valuable opportunity, believe the proposed path is
credible, and approve a concrete commitment. Lock the audience problem,
decision authority, value basis, cost, delivery condition, and ask.

Promises require proof or an explicit assumption. Keep commercial, legal, and
delivery qualifiers intact when compressing the copy.

## Narrative

Move from the audience's situation and stakes to the proposed change, proof,
economics, delivery path, risks, and a precise ask. Demonstrate value before
describing the team or feature inventory. A proposal can lead with tension,
evidence, or a future-state demonstration, but it must converge on a decision.

## Density and rhythm

Open with a small number of memorable claims. Place denser proof, economics,
and feasibility in the middle. Return to a sparse, explicit decision at the
close. In `reader` mode, keep conditions and source detail visible or in a
clearly attached appendix; in `live` mode, notes may carry the talk track.

## Visual carriers and archetypes

- before/after operating model or experience sequence;
- value chain showing who gains what and how it is measured;
- product, workflow, or customer evidence image with annotation;
- measured proof page centered on one result or case;
- unit-economics, investment-return, or payback chart;
- phased delivery path with owners, gates, dependencies, and risks;
- final ask with scope, timing, commitment, and next action.

Use product or contextual imagery when seeing the proposed change improves
belief. Use charts for economics and measured proof, diagrams for the operating
change, and tables for exact commercial or delivery terms.

## Visual grammar

Use confident scale contrast and a directional reading path. Separate promise,
proof, and commitment through surface roles rather than repeating one card
style. Rectangles may frame phases or commitments; lines should indicate flow,
handoff, or accumulation. Reserve the strongest visual role for the proposed
outcome or the decision, not for decoration.

Typography should move from a human-readable promise to concrete proof. Crops,
SVG, and native vectors should share one edge, contrast, and icon language.
Keep one or two motifs and vary silhouettes across promise, proof, economics,
and ask pages.

## Avoid

Slogan stacks, feature catalogs, invented testimonials, oversized market
numbers without method, vague asks, decorative funnels, generic success stock
photos, and a closing page that hides commercial or delivery conditions.

## Review questions

- Is the proposal framed in the audience's terms rather than the seller's?
- Is every important promise paired with proof or a stated assumption?
- Are economics, risk, responsibility, and the ask easy to find?
- Would the deck still support a decision after the meeting?
- If belief is weak, does the page need better evidence or a better carrier
  before it needs more visual polish?

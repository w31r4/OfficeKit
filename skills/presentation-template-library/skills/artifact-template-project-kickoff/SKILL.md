# Project Kickoff

## Role and communication job

Use this style when the brief matches **project kickoff, initiative launch, and implementation plan**. It is designed for
project teams, stakeholders, and cross-functional partners. A route into action: the deck gives a team a shared destination, sequence, and set of commitments. The deck should help the audience make the
change implied by the work, not merely browse attractive pages. Its natural
story arc is **why now → outcome → scope → route → commitments and risks**. It is not a good fit for **quarterly board review or market research report**; choose
another style or record the mismatch instead of forcing this grammar.

## Visual grammar

### Surface, palette, and type

- Use an open light surface and one confident accent line. Distinguish phases by position and route; reserve stronger contrast for the destination or decision.
- Write active titles, short labels, explicit verbs, and a large outcome statement. Names, owners, dates, and dependencies need a readable role of their own.
- Keep units, dates, source basis, and uncertainty adjacent to the evidence
  they qualify. A visual hierarchy must not erase the conditions behind a claim.

### Geometry, layers, and motif

- Use routes, timelines, dependency lines, role bands, and milestones. Connectors must have endpoints and labels; a destination can carry the strongest shape.
- Use native Office text, charts, tables, connectors, SVG, and images when they
  carry the page's claim. Keep important foreground content editable.
- For a full-page image, use the real scene stack: background image → tested
  scrim or contrast field → editable claim/evidence → foreground markers. Keep
  axes, labels, connectors, decision lines, and chart marks unobstructed. If the
  stack cannot be proven after rendering, use a bounded image region or refuse
  the treatment.

## Page composition

### Preferred page archetypes

Use these archetypes as a vocabulary, not fixed layouts:

1. **outcome opener** — define the audience task, scope, and one visual
   signal before adding supporting material.
2. **scope boundary** — give one dominant carrier the space needed to be
   understood, with the shortest useful explanation.
3. **roadmap route** — expose the relationship, method, or comparison with
   lines, axes, labels, or a measured table.
4. **working agreement** — state the implication, owner, condition, or next
   move; do not end on an ornamental summary.

### Information carriers

- Use timeline contours, dependency maps, role/decision tables, milestone marks, and risk paths. Avoid a row of cards when a route communicates the work better.
- Use a team, product, or place image only when it makes the change concrete; pair it with a caption, milestone, or question.
- A chart, table, photograph, SVG, or geometric composition is valid only when
  it communicates scale, sequence, comparison, context, or identity. Remove
  decorative shapes that do not support the page's conclusion.
- Prefer direct labels and short reading cues over unexplained legends or icons.
  If a framework has no meaningful relationship to show, use typography and a
  rule instead of manufacturing a diagram.

## Density and rhythm

The expected density is **medium: open with the shared outcome, increase density for route and dependencies, then close with concrete commitments.**. Move from orientation to sequence to agreement. Keep the future-facing pages energetic without turning every phase into a colored block. Do not distribute
whitespace evenly by habit; concentrate it around the title-to-body transition,
carrier edges, column gutters, and final implication. If the brief calls for a
sparse page, record what the empty field is doing.

## Style-specific playbook

- **Page skeleton.** Set the destination and reason, establish the scope boundary, draw the route, and finish with owner-backed commitments.
- **Type rhythm.** Keep the title, evidence, caption, unit, and source roles
  distinct. Let the largest type carry the reading decision, not the ornament.
- **Evidence carriers.** Use timeline contours, dependency maps, role/decision tables, milestone marks, and risk paths. Avoid a row of cards when a route communicates the work better.
- **Images and layers.** Use a team, product, or place image only when it makes the change concrete; pair it with a caption, milestone, or question. Inspect crop, contrast, and z-order after
  rendering; do not call an obscured line or label intentional without evidence.
- **Pacing.** Move from orientation to sequence to agreement. Keep the future-facing pages energetic without turning every phase into a colored block. Let adjacent pages change energy or silhouette when
  the narrative changes, while the design grammar remains recognizable.
- **Selection rule.** Choose this style when the audience needs to align on what will happen, who owns it, and how progress will be judged.

## Signature moves and variation limits

- Use a consistent route cue, but vary between timeline, dependency, and decision contours.
- Make verbs, owners, and dates more prominent than slogans.
- Let the final page answer what happens next and what would change the plan.
- If a user supplies a brand system or reference authority, it wins. This guide
  fills undefined choices and must not be blended with a conflicting style.

## Anti-patterns and refusal boundaries

- Avoid generic success badges, decorative arrows without endpoints, and equal panels for every workstream.
- Do not imply commitment without an owner, date, or acceptance condition.
- Do not let background imagery hide dependency lines or decision labels.
- Do not invent facts, sources, or visual meaning. Mark unresolved design facts
  as unresolved and ask only when the conflict changes the communication result.
- If a requested effect would hide evidence, alter an unknown native object, or
  require a fixed page clone, keep the safe portions and state the boundary.

## Calibration evidence

Inspect all four example images before composing. The four examples demonstrate destination, scope, route, and commitment pages; they are original calibration scenes for free composition. Recompose
every real page from its communication task. Never reuse their wording,
geometry, image bytes, or implied source deck.

## Review checklist

- The page has one audience task, one conclusion, and one primary visual carrier.
- Check title read-through, source/basis treatment, units, labels, and whether
  the final implication follows from the evidence.
- Text, image crop, contrast field, chart marks, connectors, and foreground
  labels survive a rendered visual check without occlusion or overflow.
- The deck varies page silhouette and density with purpose instead of repeating
  a wall of equal modules.
- Reopen the exported PPTX and structurally inspect it. Record the absolute
  path, SHA-256, and visual-review state before delivery.
